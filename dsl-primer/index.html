game-dsl · a primer

Say what and when.
The model does the how.

A tour of the primitives you write a game with. Every snippet is taken from Chicago, 1923, the first game written in the language: rackets, crews, heat and the police.

00

The idea

A game definition is a C# expression that builds data. Every lambda runs once, when the definition is built, against scope objects whose members produce expression trees. Nothing reads game state. The result is a Model, and several consumers read that one model.

The rule of thumb: a scenario never says how something happens. If you find yourself writing a loop or a flag, the mechanism belongs in the model as a named concept.

01

Entities and vocabulary Attr · Named · Rel

A kind of thing is a class. Attr<T>() is stored state; any other property is derived. Wrap a derived property in Named(...) and it becomes part of the game's vocabulary: explanations say "it is guarded" instead of spelling out the expression, and every entity sheet lists it with its definition.

02

Identity Ref · Id

A display name is written exactly once, where its identity is declared. Everything else points at the identity, so a typo is a compile error and a test makes sure no name is declared twice. Resources and relations need no name; they take it from the member that reads them.

03

Resources and relations Resource · Relation

A resource is a quantity one kind of entity holds. A relation is a directed value from one entity to another: a number, a level on a scale, or a yes/no. Either can be bounded and can drift on its own when nothing else moves it.

04

Named numbers Tune · Param

Numbers are written where they are used. A number that several rules share, or that a situation may set differently, becomes a Param. A Param can go anywhere a number can, and explanations print it with its name: "above 60 (hot heat)".

05

Flows Flow

A flow is a transfer that runs every turn for each subject. Declare it once and it runs for every outfit, nation or household that matches. It moves an amount from a source to a target, and may draw or relieve some other value on the side.

06

Pressures Pressure

A pressure is a slow squeeze. While its condition holds, it wears a value down each turn. When the value reaches its floor, something happens. This is how a game builds tension without timers or flags.

07

Events and options Event · Option

An event either rolls each turn for subjects that match its condition, or is raised by a decision, a pressure or another event. If it has options, its chooser picks one. The human gets a prompt and the AI picks by weight. One option must always be available, so nobody gets stuck.

08

Decisions Decision · Choose · Standing

A decision is something an actor can do. Choose lists what it can be done to, and Requires says when it is allowed. A personal act happens once. A standing order assigns a person and keeps working every turn until it is replaced or lapses. The menu, the reasons a choice is refused, and the AI's choices all come from these declarations.

09

Traits and involvement Trait · Modifies · Involves

A trait says what it bends, and by how much. It does not say when it applies. That comes from the mechanic: every flow, event or decision says whom it involves, and a trait counts only while its carrier is involved. Percentages add up, then apply to the base.

10

Concerns and mind Concern · Mind

The AI has no script. A concern is something an actor wants kept within bounds. Decisions say which concerns they Serve, and the further a concern is from satisfied, the harder it pulls on their scores. A mind sets how well the actor plays: how competent, how bold, how steady.

11

Endings Ending

An ending is either checked every turn against a condition, or raised by an effect. It is a victory or a defeat for its subject, and everyone else gets the opposite. The window can show how far the player is from each ending, and what works toward it.

12

Situations Situation.Over

The rules are mechanism. A situation is content: a concrete starting world with literal values, tuned numbers, and the seat the human plays. A game can have many situations. Short helper methods keep them readable, and they only call the generic Create, Set, Relate and Give.

13

The turn

You never write a game loop. The engine runs the same order every turn, for every game. You only declare things, and the engine decides when each one runs.

14

Effects and conditions

Effects are a closed set that the checker knows completely. If an effect you need doesn't exist, that's a missing domain concept: add it to the model and log the gap in the backlog. Don't build it out of loops.

When · All · set.Eachcombine effects
Let(sel, x => ...)pick once, then change it
Spawn<T>(after:, ...)bring a new thing in
Buy(from, units, at:)pay at a market price
Remove(entity)take a thing out

Conditions combine with & | !. On sets: Where, Any, None, Count, Sum, Strongest, Weakest, Random(). On values: Is, Above, Below, AtLeast, AtFloor, Has(trait), Doing(order). Add .For(Weeks(n)) and a condition must hold that many turns in a row.

  • Effects and involvement take one lambda returning one item or a list: .Effect(t => [a, b, c]).
  • Reading through nothing gives nothing, and a condition on nothing is false.
  • A relation never written reads as its declared default.
  • Illegal definitions fail to compile, or fail the checker with a message in game terms.
